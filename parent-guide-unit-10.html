This Unit at a Glance

This unit is your student’s first look at government and citizenship. It starts with why communities need a government at all, then opens the U.S. Constitution and its first words, “We the People.” Your student learns the three levels of government (local, state, and national), Florida’s own constitution and symbols, how voting and elections stay fair, and the patriotic holidays the country keeps. The unit ends with the civic virtues good citizens practice and the symbols, people, documents, and events that tell the story of the United States.

Thinking skill
Recommend with evidence. Several lessons end with a Big Question: your student picks a side, gives two reasons, and names a real example from the lesson.
Virtue lens
Service and honesty. Helping your community, and telling the truth and being fair, even when it is hard.
Big idea
The power comes from the people. A constitution sets up a government, gives it its jobs, and sets its limits. Citizens vote, serve, and practice the habits that hold a community together.

“E Pluribus Unum: out of many, one.”The motto on the Great Seal of the United States, Lesson 3.10.08

Good to know: The lessons correct a few common mix-ups. A President is not a king with a different name. The President does not run everything: local and state governments do many jobs. July 4, 1776 is the Declaration of Independence, not the Constitution (1787). Civility does not mean agreeing. A law cannot make someone volunteer. The Statue of Liberty is a symbol, not a real person. And heroes are not only Presidents.

In the Museum

The 3rd grade course is set in a museum. In this unit your student is a Civic Curator, a museum worker who takes care of the exhibits in Citizenship Hall. Opti the owl is the museum guide.

Your student fills Citizenship Hall one exhibit at a time. They hang its first label as the dark hall lights up, drop a ballot at the Constitution case, label the three-level model, light the Florida case, stamp COUNTED at the voting booth, light up the holiday wall month by month, and hang a bronze plaque at the Heart of the Hall. In the last lesson a spotlight lands on the center pedestal. Your student answers the hall’s last Big Question and closes the hall: all eight exhibits light up and fly together into one Citizen Curator badge. Out of many, one.

Lesson by Lesson

LessonWhat your student learnsWhat they write
3.10.01Week 28 Why Government Is Needed
A government is a group of people chosen to make rules and take care of a community. It has three big jobs: keeping order (fair rules), keeping people safe, and caring for the common good (things that help everyone, like a park). Taxes are the money everyone puts in to pay for things everyone shares. LobbyPress Go at a busy corner with no stop signs and watch everyone get stuck. Put up the signs and try again. GalleryFix the troubles in a model Town With No Rules. Read the first words of the Constitution, “We the People,” in the real 1787 handwriting. Drop coins from ten families into the Town Jar and see what it buys. Source: the first page of the U.S. Constitution, 1787 (National Archives)Lens: Service		 The first label in Citizenship Hall: three sentences on what a government is, one of its jobs, and a real example.
3.10.02Week 28 The U.S. Constitution and Power from the People
A constitution is the written rules that set up a government. The U.S. Constitution begins “We the People”: the government’s power comes from the people. The people vote to choose their leaders, and every leader, the President too, must follow the Constitution. A President is not a king: a President is chosen by a vote and serves for four years at a time. LobbyWatch the Recess Boss pick the game for everyone, then let the class vote instead. GalleryFlip a town square from a king’s rule to “We the People.” Sort six cards onto “King” or “President.” Move a lamp down the real first page of the Constitution. Source: the first page of the U.S. Constitution, 1787 (National Archives)Lens: Honesty		 A ballot for the Constitution case: a side, two reasons, and evidence, in 3 to 5 sentences.
3.10.03Week 29 Local, State, and National Government
Government has three levels. Local (your city or county; a mayor; city hall) picks up the trash and runs the library. State (Florida; the governor; Tallahassee) gives out driver’s licenses. National (the whole country; the President; Washington, D.C.) makes the money used in every state. Some jobs, like running a school, get help from more than one level. LobbyAddress an envelope to your own home: town, state, country. Each line lights up one level. GalleryRide an elevator up a three-level model and send each job to the right floor. Switch on three layers of a drawn school to see which level does what. Match three real government buildings to their levels. Source: photos of the Florida Capitol, the U.S. Capitol, and Tampa City HallLens: Service		 Plaques for the three-level model: 3 to 5 sentences that name all three levels and why they work together.
3.10.04Week 29 The Florida Constitution and Florida Symbols
In 1513, the Spanish explorer Juan Ponce de León named the land La Florida, “the flowered place.” The Florida Constitution sets up Florida’s state government, the same way the U.S. Constitution sets up the national one. Florida’s symbols include the state seal (the sun, a sabal palm, a Seminole woman, a steamboat), the sabal palm, the mockingbird, and the orange blossom. Mary McLeod Bethune started a school in Daytona Beach in 1904 with $1.50 and five girls, and served her country. LobbySail to an old-style map of Florida in 1513 and spell its name with flowers. GalleryOpen Florida’s own constitution, page by page. Find the three things Florida fixed on its state seal in 1985. Light up three objects that tell Mary McLeod Bethune’s story. Source: an 1879 engraving of Florida’s old state seal (used 1868 to 1985)Lens: Honesty		 A recommendation for the symbol that best stands for Florida, the sabal palm or the state seal, with two reasons and evidence.
3.10.05Week 30 Voting and Elections
A vote is one person’s choice. An election is when many people vote to pick something or someone. Three rules keep it fair: one citizen, one vote; a secret ballot (you vote in private, so no one can watch or push you); and the votes are counted carefully. To vote in the United States, a person must be a citizen, 18 or older, and registered. The President and Florida’s governor are each elected for four years. LobbyClose the curtain on a voting booth and watch a pushy crowd have to wait. GallerySwitch the three fairness rules off one at a time on a working election model and see what goes wrong. Check four visitors in at the voting desk. Tap the stars, stripes, and red X on the U.S. and Florida flags. Source: a real voting booth in Summit, New Jersey, on Election Day, November 6, 2012Lens: Service		 A label for the voting booth: one fair rule and what would go wrong without it, in 3 to 5 sentences.
3.10.06Week 30 Patriotic Holidays and Observances
A patriotic holiday is a day set aside to honor the country, its people, or an idea. Some days celebrate, like Independence Day (July 4) and Constitution Day (September 17). Some days remember, like Memorial Day, which honors those who died serving. Veterans Day (November 11) honors everyone who has served. The unit’s timeline also includes Martin Luther King Jr. Day, Presidents’ Day, Flag Day, and Juneteenth. All three levels of government help with holidays. LobbySkip ahead 100 years and watch a holiday wall go dark and forgotten, then light it back up. GalleryHang eight objects on a January-to-December wall and choose a bright or a soft lamp for each. Plan a Veterans Day parade at all three levels of government. Count flags in a real photo from Arlington National Cemetery. Source: Flags In at Arlington National Cemetery, Virginia, May 26, 2016Lens: Service		 A recommendation for The Big Question: which matters more, days that celebrate or days that remember, with two reasons and one holiday as evidence.
3.10.07Week 31 Civic Virtues
A civic virtue is a habit a citizen practices on purpose to help the community. The four in this lesson: civility (treating people with respect, even when you disagree), cooperation (working together), volunteerism (choosing to help without being told to), and responsibility (doing what you said you would do). Anyone can practice them, even a third grader. A law cannot make someone volunteer: a constitution gives government its jobs and its limits, and volunteering is always a choice. LobbyTurn over four dusty bronze plaques and rub off the dust to read each word. GalleryPolish the plaques through a week of eight small choices. Help on Martin Luther King Jr. Day, a national day of service, at a city park, a Florida state park, and a national park. Hang virtue tags on a real photo of park volunteers. Source: volunteers at Tule Springs Fossil Beds National Monument, Nevada, April 11, 2015 (National Park Service)Lens: Service		 One evidence question at the Day of Service station, and a plaque label on the virtue their community needs most, in 3 to 5 sentences.
3.10.08Week 31 Symbols, Individuals, Documents, and Events
Curators tell a country’s story with four kinds of evidence: symbols (what we honor, like the flag and the Statue of Liberty), individuals (who shaped us, like George Washington and Rosa Parks), documents (what we believe, like the Declaration of Independence and the Constitution), and events (what tested us, like the Civil War). Rosa Parks showed heroism when she refused to give up her bus seat in 1955. The Tuskegee Airmen, the first Black pilots in the U.S. military, showed patriotism in World War II. The motto on the Great Seal, E Pluribus Unum, means “out of many, one.” LobbyIn a dark hall, guess five objects on the center pedestal from their shadows, then light each one up. GallerySort eight cards onto four pillars that hold up a roof marked UNITED STATES, then pull one pillar out and watch the roof tip. Put Rosa Parks’ story in order and fly the Red Tails’ mission. Hunt for the number 13 on the real Great Seal. Source: the Great Seal of the United States, first used in 1782Lens: Honesty		 One evidence question at Two Brave Choices, and The Big Question: symbols and documents, or individuals and events? Closing the hall earns the Citizen Curator badge.

Each Workroom also has a short word puzzle about one word part: civ (citizen), stitu (to set up), loc (place), flor (flower), elect (to choose), memor (remembering), volunt (willing, by choice), and uni (one).

Ask at Home

Better than “How was school?”
  1. Why does a community need a government?Listen for: to keep order, keep people safe, and care for the common good.
  2. Where does the government’s power come from?Listen for: the people (“We the People”), who choose leaders by voting.
  3. Is a President the same as a king?Listen for: no; a President is chosen by a vote, serves for a set time, and must follow the Constitution too.
  4. Who picks up our trash? Who gives out driver’s licenses? Who makes our money?Listen for: local government, state government (Florida), national government.
  5. What does the name Florida mean?Listen for: “the flowered place,” named by Juan Ponce de León in 1513.
  6. Why does a voting booth have a curtain?Listen for: so each person votes in private and no one can watch or push them (a secret ballot).
  7. What is the difference between Memorial Day and Veterans Day?Listen for: Memorial Day remembers those who died serving; Veterans Day honors everyone who has served.
  8. Can you be civil to someone you disagree with?Listen for: yes; civility means respect, not agreeing.
  9. What does E Pluribus Unum mean, and where is it?Listen for: “out of many, one,” on the Great Seal of the United States (and on coins and the dollar bill).

Try at Home

5 minutes
  • Three levels hunt. On a drive or a walk, point out something your city runs, something Florida runs, and something the national government runs (a post office, a mailbox, a dollar bill).
  • Family vote. Pick a movie or a dinner with a secret ballot: everyone writes a choice on paper, folds it, and one person counts.
  • Find the motto. Look at a penny or the back of a dollar bill and find E Pluribus Unum.
  • One small choice. Pick one civic virtue for the week and do one action that shows it.

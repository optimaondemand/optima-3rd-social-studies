This Unit at a Glance

This is the last unit of 3rd grade social studies, and it is your student’s own exhibit. In four lessons your student plans an exhibit about one place they studied this year, builds it into slides with real pictures, presents it out loud, and reflects on the whole year. The exhibit has a welcome slide and four parts: a map, a source, a community connection, and a virtue. In the last lesson your student adds a sixth slide: Who I became.

Thinking skill
Show, then tell. Pick real evidence, label it clearly, and explain it out loud to a visitor.
Virtue lens
Service, courtesy, and honesty. Making your work easy for others, telling the truth about what you know, and using what you know to help.
Big idea
The real exhibit is you. The rooms and badges were practice for four habits a young geographer-citizen keeps for life.

“This museum was never really about the exhibits. The real exhibit is each one of you.”The museum director, Lesson 3.11.04

Good to know: The lessons correct a few common mix-ups. More words do not make a slide better. A good guide does not have to know everything: saying “I’m not sure” is honest. One photo shows some things, but it can’t prove everything about a place. And being a geographer-citizen doesn’t end when the school year does.

🏛️

In the Museum

The 3rd grade course is set in a museum, and this unit takes place in its last room, the Grand Exhibition Hall. Your student is an Exhibit Designer, then a Museum Guide, and at the very end a Young Geographer-Citizen. Opti the owl is still the museum guide.

Your student pins up an exhibit plan, builds it into slides, hangs them on a panel, and presents them to visitors who clap at the end. On the last day the director gathers everyone, a spotlight finds a mirror marked THE REAL EXHIBIT, and then the museum doors open to the whole community. Your student earns the Young Geographer-Citizen badge.

Lesson by Lesson

LessonWhat your student learnsWhat they write
3.11.01Week 32 Review: See the Place, Hear the Voices, Follow the Story
Your student looks back at the whole year, room by room, and plans the exhibit they will build. A region is a group of places that are close together or have something in common, and one region can reach across a country’s border. A strong exhibit has four parts: a map, a source, a community connection, and a virtue. LobbyPut eight badges from the year back on a floor plan of the museum, then light it up all the way to the Grand Exhibition Hall. GalleryTidy a crowded Great Plains panel: keep a piece or take it down, and say why. Look for the border between the United States and Canada on a real picture of the Great Lakes taken from space. Source: the Great Lakes from space (NASA, 2000)Lens: Service		 An exhibit plan: one of six places (Florida, the Northeast, the Great Plains, the Southwest, the West, or Mexico and the Caribbean), a map, a source, a community fact, and a virtue, in 3 to 5 sentences.
3.11.02Week 32 Build the Final Community-in-the-World Exhibit
A slide is one page of a presentation: one real picture and a few words. A strong slide names the place and says why it matters, in words a visitor can read fast. Too many words make a visitor walk away. Courtesy for an exhibit designer means making the work easy to read. LobbyUnroll the plan and slide each pick into one of five empty frames on the panel. GalleryFix a weak slide for a visitor who asks one question at a time (What am I looking at? A map of where? Why should I care?). Sources: real pictures from the place your student chose, and maps of that regionLens: Courtesy		 Five slides in the Exhibit Studio (Welcome, Map, Source, Community, Virtue), 6 to 25 words each, then hung on the panel. The slides can be downloaded as a PowerPoint file.
3.11.03Week 33 Present the Evidence: Map, Source, and Virtue
A walk-through is what a guide says out loud at a panel, in five steps that match the five slides: greet, show the map, explain the source, point to the community, and end with the virtue and “Do you have any questions?” An honest guide says “I’m not sure” instead of making something up, and says what a source shows without stretching it. LobbyTap each slide on the panel and see which step of the walk-through goes with it. GalleryCatch the polished line: a helper guide talks about a Southwest panel, one line at a time, and your student stops the lines that are stretched or made up. Then look at the real picture from their own Source slide and pick the honest sentence about it. Source: the picture on your student’s own Source slideLens: Honesty		 What I’ll say under each of the five slides (at least 5 words each), practice out loud (recording is optional), then present all five slides to the visitors. Evidence questions: a question I can’t answer, my hardest step, and what my source can’t show.
3.11.04Week 33 Final Historian-Citizen Reflection
The director tells every Exhibit Designer that the museum was never really about the exhibits: the real exhibit is each student. A young geographer-citizen has four habits: see the world carefully, use evidence honestly, make wise choices, and serve the common good. Every room of the museum trained one of them. LobbyHear the director’s words one line at a time, until a spotlight finds a mirror marked THE REAL EXHIBIT, with your student’s name. GallerySort six badges into four wings of the museum, one for each habit. Find North America, the Caribbean, and Florida on a real picture of Earth from space, and say what a picture from space can’t show (borders and names). Source: Earth from space, the Western Hemisphere (NASA, 2007)Lens: Service		 The Historian-Citizen Reflection (one prompt for each habit), a sixth slide, Who I became, three last questions, and the last Chronicle. Then the museum doors open.

Each Workroom also has a short word puzzle about one word part: cur (care), pict (paint), aud (hear), and fin (end).

Ask at Home

Better than “How was school?”
  1. Can you show me your six slides and walk me through them?Listen for: the five steps: a greeting, the map, the source, the community, and the virtue with “Do you have any questions?”, plus slide six, Who I became.
  2. What are the four parts of a strong exhibit?Listen for: a map, a source, a community connection, and a virtue.
  3. What makes a slide strong?Listen for: one real picture and a few clear words that name the place and say why it matters; not too many words.
  4. What should a museum guide say when a visitor asks something they don’t know?Listen for: “Good question. I’m not sure, but here is what I do know.” No making things up.
  5. What does your source show? What can’t it show?Listen for: what is really in the picture, and one thing it can’t tell a visitor.
  6. What can’t you see in a picture of Earth from space?Listen for: the border lines and the names of places: people draw those on maps.
  7. What are the four habits of a young geographer-citizen? Which one did you grow in most?Listen for: see the world carefully, use evidence honestly, make wise choices, serve the common good.
  8. What did the museum director say the real exhibit was?Listen for: the students themselves, and who they became this year.
  9. What does the word part aud mean? Can you name an aud word?Listen for: hear: audience, audio, audible, auditorium.

What Gets Turned In

  • Each lesson: the Curator Task (the exhibit plan, the five slides, what I’ll say, or the final reflection with slide six), a Curator’s Chronicle sentence, and the evidence or reflection questions
  • The slides are saved in the browser on that computer. A PowerPoint file or a video can be added in Canvas if the teacher asks.
  • End of the unit: a 10-question Unit 11 quiz in Canvas
  • Copied or downloaded, then submitted in Canvas

Done well looks like: a real picture and a few clear words on every slide, the place named, a reason that starts with “because,” and honest words: what the evidence shows, and “I’m not sure” when that is true.

Try at Home

5 minutes
  • Be the audience. Let your student present all six slides to the family, then ask one question they might not know. Listen for an honest answer.
  • Shows, not proves. Look at a family photo together. Say one thing it shows, and one thing it can’t tell you.
  • Habit of the week. Pick one of the four habits and do one thing that shows it, like helping a neighbor (serve the common good).
  • Find home from space. Look at a globe or a world map and find North America, the Caribbean, and Florida.

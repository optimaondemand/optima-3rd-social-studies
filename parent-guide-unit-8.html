This Unit at a Glance

Last unit, your student studied the five regions of the United States one at a time. This short unit puts them all on one table. First, your student gathers evidence from all five regions and sees that no region has everything it needs on its own. Then the council takes on a hard question about one resource, fresh water: which region needs it most, and how can everyone share it?

Thinking skill
Evidence or opinion? Evidence is a fact anyone can check, like “Phoenix gets about 7 inches of rain in a year.” An opinion is what someone thinks or feels, like “Deserts are boring.”
Virtue lens
Honesty. An honest council reports what the evidence really shows and looks at both sides, even when the evidence points away from the answer it hoped for.
Big idea
Regions need each other. Every region gets something from the others. When a resource is limited, like the water in one river, people have to share it and use it wisely.

“No region has everything it needs on its own.”What the council ledger shows, Lesson 3.08.01

Good to know: The lessons correct a few common mix-ups. Evidence is not the same as an opinion. Water under the ground can run out: rain refills it only a little each year. Rivers don’t always reach the ocean: so much water is taken out of the Colorado River that most years it dries up before it reaches the sea. And deserts do have water, but much of it comes from far away, through rivers, canals, and wells.

In the Museum

The 3rd grade course is set in a museum. In this unit your student is a Council Curator, a museum worker who helps the museum council make careful choices. Opti the owl is the museum guide.

A side door off the United States Regions Gallery opens to the Regional Council Chamber: a long oak table with five seats, one for each region. In the first lesson, evidence cards from all five alcoves have spilled across the table. Your student sorts them, fills in the council ledger, and seals the evidence folder with wax. In the second lesson, a sealed envelope holds the council’s question: FRESH WATER. Your student follows the Colorado River, weighs the evidence, and writes a recommendation. When the council votes, the placards flip, the stamp comes down, and a new door opens a crack: the Marketplace Hall.

3.08.02Week 23 Council Chamber: Which Region Needs This Resource?
The Southwest gets the least rain (Phoenix gets only about 7 inches a year) and depends on the Colorado River. Seven states and Mexico share that river. So much water is taken out that most years it dries up before it reaches the sea, and Lake Mead, the giant lake behind Hoover Dam, has shrunk during many dry years. Words to Know

Use them at home!
councila group of people who meet to talk about a problem and decide what to do
evidencefacts you can see and check that show something is true
opinionwhat someone thinks or feels; it can’t be checked like a fact
honestytelling the truth and reporting what really happened
irrigationbringing water to crops with pipes, ditches, or sprinklers
aquifera layer of rock and sand underground that holds water, like the Ogallala Aquifer under the Great Plains
Colorado Rivera big river that starts as snow in the Rocky Mountains and flows through the Southwest and the West toward the sea in Mexico
reservoira lake made by a dam, used to store water, like Lake Mead behind Hoover Dam
canala dug-out path that carries water from one place to another
droughta long time with much less rain or snow than usual
sharea part of something that is split among people
recommendationadvice about what someone should do, with reasons

Ask at Home

Better than “How was school?”
  1. What is the difference between evidence and an opinion?Listen for: evidence is a fact anyone can check; an opinion is what someone thinks or feels.
  2. What does our region get from other regions?Listen for: one real thing (a food, fuel, or water) and the region it comes from.
  3. Why are some fields on the Great Plains shaped like circles?Listen for: a sprinkler turns in a circle, watering crops with water pumped from underground.
  4. Can water under the ground run out?Listen for: yes, if people pump out more than rain puts back.
  5. What happens to the Colorado River before it reaches the sea?Listen for: seven states and Mexico take so much water that most years it dries up first.
  6. Which region did you recommend, and what was your evidence?Listen for: a region name and two facts, not just “because I like it.”

Done well looks like: real facts that can be checked (numbers, places, resources), not just opinions; the region’s name; and an honest reason. For the recommendation, one real way people could share or save water.

Try at Home

5 minutes
  • Evidence or opinion? Take turns saying one sentence about your town. The other person says “evidence” or “opinion.”
  • Where does our water come from? Look up your town’s water source together: a river, a lake, a reservoir, or wells.
  • Water detective. Count how many times water is used in your home before lunch. Name one way to save some.
  • From far away. Pick one food at home and guess which region it came from.

Word Parts
Latin word part: stitu, "to set up, to put in place"

The Latin word part stitu means "to set up" or "to put in place." You can find it in the middle of all four words below. When you constitute something, you set it up on purpose. That is what the writers of the U.S. Constitution did: they set up a whole new government.

📖

constitution: the written rules that set up a government · constitute: to make up or form something · institute: to set something up or start it · substitute: to put one thing in place of another

Learn the Story
📖
A Country Built From a Piece of Paper
Why 1787 still matters

In the summer of 1787, leaders from across the brand-new United States traveled to a city called Philadelphia. The country had been free from Britain for only a few years, and things were not going well. Each state had its own rules and its own money. Some states even taxed goods that came from other states. The leaders met inside one hot building all summer and wrote down a plan: the Constitution.

A constitution is the written rules that set up a government. The U.S. Constitution tells who makes the laws, who carries them out, and who decides if they are fair. A few years later, in 1791, the people added a list of their rights, called the Bill of Rights. The Constitution did something new for its time: it said the country's power would come from the people, not from a king.

That is what the first three words mean: "We the People." The Constitution does not begin with the name of a ruler. It begins with everybody. The people make the country, the people choose the leaders, and the leaders work for the people. We call this idea power from the people, or self-government.

How do the people use their power? They vote. In an election, each voter gets one vote, and all the votes are counted. That is how Americans choose the President and the lawmakers in Congress. When people do not vote, other people make the choice for them. That is why voting is so important.

Here is the part curators love: even the President of the United States has to follow the Constitution. The most powerful person in the country must obey the same rules as everyone else. That is how a constitution protects everyone: it sits above everyone.

Doing our part: Power from the people works best when the people do their part. Good citizens show civility (being polite, even when they disagree), cooperation (working together), and volunteerism (helping others without being paid).

🤔

Pause & Think: Think about the words "the most powerful person still has to follow the rules." Why is that idea so important for keeping a country fair?

The Big Question
A new country is being built. Should one person decide all the rules, or should the people decide together?
👑 Side A: One Strong Leader

A king or queen could make decisions quickly, with no arguing. Everyone would know who's in charge. If the leader is wise, the country runs smoothly. There would be no long debates or slow votes when the country needs to act fast.

🤝 Side B: Power from the People

The people vote and choose their own leaders. No single person has all the power, so no single person can become a tyrant. Even the top leader has to follow the same rules everyone else does. Decisions take longer, but they belong to everybody, not just one family.

The leaders in 1787 chose Side B. They wrote the U.S. Constitution to set up a government with power from the people. Look at both sides carefully. Notice what each one gains, and what each one loses. In the last tab, you will write your own recommendation, using evidence from this lesson to support your choice.

⏳

Then & Now: In 1787, only some people could vote, and that was not fair. Over the years, the people changed the Constitution (these changes are called amendments) so that more people could vote. Today, almost every citizen 18 or older can vote. The Constitution is still doing its job, and the people kept making it better.

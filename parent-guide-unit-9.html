This Unit at a Glance

This unit is your student’s first look at economics: how people choose, trade, buy, and sell. It starts with scarcity (there is not enough of everything for everyone), which is why people make choices and why they trade. Then come goods and services, buyers and sellers, what makes good money, the money our neighbors use, and the trade routes that carry goods between regions. The unit ends with weighing costs and benefits, and with the two virtues that hold a marketplace together: honesty and responsibility.

Thinking skill
Trade and Choice. For any trade or purchase, your student names four things: the choice, the scarcity or cost, the benefit, and the consequence.
Virtue lens
Honesty and responsibility. Telling the truth about what you sell and pay, and doing what you said you would do. The unit also touches courage, courtesy, and self-governance.
Big idea
Scarcity leads to choices and to trade. No person, town, or region has everything. People choose, trade, and use money to get what they need, and it only works when they can trust each other.

“Every choice has two sides. What you get is a benefit. What you give up is a cost.”The Shiny Toy Stand, Lesson 3.09.07

Good to know: The lessons correct a few common mix-ups. Scarce does not mean all gone. A trade does not always need money. Food does not start at the store. Two countries can both use “dollars” that are not the same money. Free does not mean no cost. And a seller who cheats does not come out ahead for long.

In the Museum

The 3rd grade course is set in a museum. In this unit your student is a Trade & Choice Curator, a museum worker who helps visitors understand how people choose and trade. Opti the owl is the museum guide.

The door from the Council Chamber opens to the Marketplace Hall, and your student fills it one exhibit at a time. They open the hall with its first panel, slide two trade tables together, hang signs over the back-wall booths, fill the money case and then widen it for our neighbors’ money, add a shipping tag to the trade routes map, and stamp DECIDED on the Decision Board. In the last lesson they write the Wise Trader plaque and hang it in a gold frame on the back wall. Every exhibit lights up from the front doors to the back wall, and your student earns the Wise Trader badge.

Lesson by Lesson

LessonWhat your student learnsWhat they write
3.09.01Week 24 Scarcity and Choice
Scarcity means there is not enough of something for everyone who wants it. Scarce does not have to mean all gone. When something is scarce, people have to choose, and every choice gives something up. Time is scarce too: every day has only 24 hours. LobbyHand out 2 loaves of bread to 3 families at the bread booth. One family is left waiting. GalleryFill a market basket by picking one good from each table and watching the other go to the “given up” shelf. Study a real ration book from World War II. Fit a whole day into a 24-hour ring. Source: a War Ration Book from 1943Lens: Responsibility		 The Intro Panel for the Marketplace Hall: three sentences of their own about scarcity, why people choose, and what gets given up.
3.09.02Week 24 How Scarcity Results in Trade
A trade is when two sides each give something and each get something back. A town by the sea has plenty of fish and not enough grain. A valley farm has plenty of grain and no fish. When they trade, both are better off. People do not always need money to trade: trading goods for goods is called barter. LobbyTap the HAS and NEEDS tags on two tables, one from the coast and one from the valley. GalleryLoad two carts and send them down the Trade Road until both pantries are full. Study photos of the Hubbell Trading Post in Arizona, where Navajo families traded wool and rugs for flour, coffee, and cloth. Sort six cards: a trade, or not a trade? Source: Hubbell Trading Post, Ganado, Arizona (National Park Service)Lens: Honesty		 A Trade and Choice panel: four sentences that name the choice, the scarcity, the benefit, and the consequence of one trade.
3.09.03Week 25 Goods, Services, Buyers, and Sellers
Goods are things you can hold, like bread or apples. A service is help or work someone does for you, like a haircut or fixing a shoe. The buyer pays. The seller sells the good or does the work. One person can be both: a baker sells bread, then pays the barber for a haircut. LobbySend four visitors, each with a wish card, to the booth that can help. GalleryFollow Max to five stops. Goods drop into his backpack; services change Max (a haircut, a fixed sneaker, a walked dog). Study two real shop photos from the 1930s. Follow coins around a ring of five sellers until everyone has been a buyer and a seller. Source: apples for sale on a sidewalk and a shoemaker in his shop, about 1939		 Four booth labels in their own words: goods, services, buyer, and seller.
3.09.04Week 25 Characteristics of Money
Money is something people agree to use to buy goods and services. Good money follows four rules: it lasts (durable), it is easy to carry (portable), it is easy to count out (divisible), and people trust it. A paper dollar works because people trust that it is worth a dollar. LobbyTry to trade a goat for a loaf of bread through a chain of sellers. Then buy the bread in one stop with two coins. GalleryRun a gold coin, a paper bill, a cowrie shell, and a block of salt through four tests. The salt melts in the rain. Study real money kept in museums. Flip a “trust” switch and watch the same $5 bill stop working. Source: a 1795 silver dollar, cowrie shells, and an 1861 $5 billLens: Courage		 A money case panel: would a chocolate bar make good money? Yes or no and a “because” for each of the four rules, then a two-sentence answer.
3.09.05Week 26 Currencies of the United States, Canada, Mexico, and the Caribbean
Currency is the kind of money a country uses. The United States uses the U.S. dollar, Canada uses the Canadian dollar, and Mexico uses the Mexican peso. The Caribbean has many currencies: Jamaica has the Jamaican dollar, and eight islands share the Eastern Caribbean dollar. Two countries can both say “dollar” and still have different money. LobbyTry to pay for a snack with U.S. dollars in Toronto and in Mexico City. Each seller kindly names the money used there. GalleryTake a four-stop trip, swapping U.S. dollars at an exchange booth and paying with the right money at each stop. Study three old coins and bills that crossed borders. Tap islands on a Caribbean map to see which money each one uses. Source: a silver peso made in Mexico City in 1796, with a 1795 U.S. silver dollar and a 1776 paper billLens: Courtesy		 Neighbor panels: a sentence each about the money of Canada, Mexico, and the Caribbean, and a closing sentence that uses one of the rules of good money.
3.09.06Week 26 Trade Routes and Regional Resources
A trade route is the path goods travel from a region with plenty to a region where they are scarce. Orange trees cannot live through Canada’s cold winters, so Canada trades for oranges from Florida. Trucks, trains, and cargo ships carry goods on routes like these every day. The store is the last stop, not the start. LobbyPlant wheat and an orange tree on a farm in Canada, then move to January and see what the cold does. GalleryDrag five crates (timber, oranges, wheat, avocados, bananas) to the region that needs each one, and pick the truck, train, or ship that carries it. Study a photo of a banana boat being loaded in Jamaica. Find out where five breakfast foods grew. Source: a steamship loading bananas in Port Antonio, Jamaica, before 1921Lens: Honesty		 A shipping tag for one trade route: the choice, the scarcity, the benefit, and the consequence.
3.09.07Week 27 Marketplace Decision: Costs and Benefits
A cost is what you give up or put in when you make a choice. It can be money, time, effort, or space. A benefit is what you gain. A wise decision weighs both sides first. Even something free has costs: a free puppy still needs walks, food, cleanup, and a bed. LobbyGrab a shiny $5 toy, then find you cannot buy the lunch snack you came for. GallerySort eight tiles on the Decision Board into costs and benefits of building a town harbor, weigh each one, vote, and watch the town for ten years. Study a photo of Boston’s Fish Pier being finished. Tap the hidden costs around a free puppy. Source: the new Fish Pier in Boston, 1914 to 1915Lens: Self-Governance		 One evidence question at the Free Puppy booth, then their own Decision Board: a family has $20 for fruit and vegetables or a board game. At least two costs, two benefits, and a decision in two to three sentences.
3.09.08Week 27 Economic Virtues: Honesty and Responsibility
Honesty is telling the truth about what you have, what you owe, and what you did. Responsibility is doing what you said you would do, even when it gets hard. A market only works when people can trust each other. A seller who cheats may win for a day, but buyers do not come back. Honesty matters even when nobody is watching. LobbyVisit an egg stand with no seller and a jar for the money. See the stand one week later if every buyer pays, and if nobody does. GalleryLook inside five pairs of booths in two markets, one honest and one not, then run them for a month. Study a 1917 photo of city scale inspectors in Seattle. Be the inspector: test four sellers’ scales with a 1 pound weight. Source: Seattle scale inspectors with the scales they took away, January 1917Lens: Responsibility		 One evidence question at the Scale Inspector station, two more on the Exhibit Floor, and the Wise Trader plaque: four sentences that use four unit words and tell how a Wise Trader shows honesty and responsibility. Hanging it earns the Wise Trader badge.

Each Workroom also has a short word puzzle about one word part: un (not), ex (out), vend (to sell), port (carry), inter (between), trans (across), bene (good), and spond or spons (promise).

Ask at Home

Better than “How was school?”
  1. What does scarcity mean? Does it mean all gone?Listen for: not enough for everyone who wants it; there can still be some left.
  2. When you picked one thing at the store, what did you give up?Listen for: the other thing the same money could have bought.
  3. Why do people trade?Listen for: each side has plenty of one thing and not enough of another, and both end up better off.
  4. Is a haircut a good or a service? What about a loaf of bread?Listen for: a haircut is a service (work someone does for you); bread is a good (you can hold it).
  5. What makes good money?Listen for: it lasts, it is easy to carry, it is easy to count out, and people trust it.
  6. Is a Canadian dollar the same as a U.S. dollar?Listen for: no; each country has its own money, even when the name is the same.
  7. Where did our orange juice (or bananas) come from?Listen for: a warm place far away, and a trade route that carried it here. Not “the store.”
  8. Can something free still have a cost?Listen for: yes: time, effort, or space, like walking and feeding a free puppy.
  9. Why does honesty matter when nobody is watching?Listen for: a stand or a market only stays open when people can trust each other.

Try at Home

5 minutes
  • Good or service? On your next errand, take turns pointing out goods (things you can hold) and services (work someone does for you).
  • Where did it grow? Read the sticker or label on one fruit. Find that place on a map and trace the route to your kitchen.
  • Two sides. Before a small family choice, name one cost and one benefit of each option out loud.
  • Look for the seal. At a grocery store scale or a gas pump, look for the inspection sticker that says the scale or pump was checked.

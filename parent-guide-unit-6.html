This Unit at a Glance

Your student zooms in from the whole world to one region: Canada, the United States, Mexico, and the Caribbean. First they learn where the four places are. Then they read the land itself: mountains, rivers, plains, and islands. Then they look at what the land gives people, like wood, wheat, water, fish, copper, and oil, and which of those can grow back. The VR day visits a Haudenosaunee longhouse.

Geography skill
Read two kinds of maps. A political map shows the countries and borders. A physical map shows the land: brown is high, green is low, blue is water.
Virtue lens
Courtesy in 3.06.01 and 3.06.02: say and spell every place name with care. Honesty in 3.06.05: tell what a resource is good for and one thing that is hard about it.
Big idea
Renewable or not? Trees, wheat, fresh water, and fish can grow back or refill if people take care of them. Oil and copper took millions of years to form. No one can make more.

“Real land doesn’t come with lines on it. People draw the lines on maps.”From Lesson 3.06.01, after the space picture

Good to know: The lessons correct a few common mix-ups. “America” is not the name of one country. The Americas means North America and South America together. Green on a physical map means low land, not grass. Oil did not come from dinosaurs. Most of it formed from tiny sea plants and animals buried long ago. On the no-headset VR timeline, one card says African peoples were brought here starting in the 1500s, and that most came against their will.

In the Museum

The 3rd grade course is set in a museum. Your student is a Regional Curator, a museum worker who takes care of one part of the world and tells visitors about it. Opti the owl is the museum guide.

The class just finished the World Map Hall. Now a new room opens: the North America & Caribbean Gallery. In Week 15, your student hangs the country labels on the big wall map, then sorts a cart of new labels for mountains, rivers, plains, and islands. In Week 17, six crates arrive at the museum’s loading dock, each holding a natural resource. Every lesson ends with your student writing a label for visitors to read.

3.06.01Week 15 Canada, the United States, Mexico, and the Caribbean
Canada is farthest north. The United States is in the middle. Mexico is to the south. The Caribbean is many islands in a warm sea, south of Florida and east of Mexico. LobbyTurn a globe made from NASA pictures to North America, zoom in, and open the gallery doors. GalleryHang four labels on a wall map using clues and the compass rose. Turn over four postcards (Moraine Lake in Canada, Old Faithful in the United States, Copper Canyon in Mexico, Trunk Bay in the U.S. Virgin Islands) and pin each one where the photo was taken. Slide map lines onto a picture from space. Then a size puzzle: Canada and the United States are about the same size, but the United States has about 8 times as many people. Source: North America from space (NASA)Lens: Courtesy		 2 evidence questions, a Gallery Welcome Label (3–4 sentences): name all four places, say where each one is with direction words, and give one reason they are studied together.
3.06.02Week 15 Physical Features of North America & the Caribbean
The Rocky Mountains run down the west side. The older, lower Appalachians run down the east. The Great Plains are wide and flat in the middle. The Mississippi River flows south to the Gulf of Mexico, and the Rio Grande forms part of the U.S.–Mexico border. The Caribbean is mostly islands, like Cuba and Hispaniola. LobbyLift the cloth on six labels and put each one in the right drawer: mountains, rivers, plains, or islands. GalleryTilt the Relief Table so the land stands up, cut a slice across the United States, and “walk” it from west to east. Read a real physical map with a color key, then turn on the borders and see that the Rockies keep going into Canada. Make it rain on the Rocky Mountains and watch four rivers carry the water east, south, west, and north to the sea. Source: a physical map made for the lessonLens: Courtesy		 3 evidence questions, a Physical Features Display Label (3–4 sentences): a mountain range, a river, and one more feature, where each one is, and why the land matters.
3.06.05Week 17 Natural Resources and How People Use Them
A natural resource is something useful that comes from nature. Renewable resources can grow back or refill if people take care of them. Nonrenewable resources are gone for a very long time once they are used up. LobbyOpen six crates: timber, wheat, fresh water, a fish (Alaska pollock), copper ore, and oil. Each one shows what it becomes: paper, bread, faucet water, fish sticks, charger wires, and gasoline. GalleryThe Time-Lapse Case: guess, use the resources, then drag a time lever to 1 year, 30 years, and 1,000 years. The forest and one fish tank come back. The other tank (every fish caught) and the oil stay empty. Then three real photos of the Great Plains from 1920, 1936 (the Dust Bowl), and 1974, a wind test with bare soil and soil with grass, and sorting each object into one of two glass cases. Source: three Great Plains photos, 1920–1974Lens: Honesty		 2 evidence questions, an Honest Exhibit Label (3–4 sentences) for one resource: what it is, where it’s found, how people use it, and one honest limit.

Ask at Home

Better than “How was school?”
  1. Where are Canada, the United States, Mexico, and the Caribbean?Listen for: Canada in the north, the United States in the middle, Mexico to the south, and the Caribbean islands to the southeast.
  2. Can you see the borders in a picture from space?Listen for: no. People agree on where borders go and draw them on maps.
  3. Canada and the United States are about the same size. Which has more people?Listen for: the United States, about 8 times as many.
  4. Which way do rivers flow?Listen for: downhill, from high land to the sea. The Mackenzie River even flows north, to the Arctic Ocean.
  5. Why did only one fish tank fill up again?Listen for: people left some grown-up fish, and they had babies. In the other tank, every fish was caught.
  6. Is oil renewable?Listen for: no. It took millions of years to form, and no one can make more.

Coming Up: Cultures That Settled the Region

The gallery isn’t only about land. It’s about people, too. In Lesson 3.06.06, a row of culture cases opens, each holding something a different group of people brought to this region: a longhouse, a Mayan pyramid, a Spanish mission, a French shield, and a drum. The story turns from what the land gives to who came here and what they brought with them. The longhouse VR day goes with this lesson.

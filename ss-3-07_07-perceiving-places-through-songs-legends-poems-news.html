🏛️ Virtue Spotlight: Courtesy
Stories belong to the people who tell them. A folk song from the Midwest was sung by real cowboys on real cattle drives. Hopi stories have been told by Hopi families for hundreds of years. Courtesy at the Listening Station means we slow down, listen first, and treat each story as a gift from the people who made it.

🔍 TEACHING · EXPLORE
How Stories Tell Us About Places
🎬
Teacher recording notes: Explain the kinds of stories at the station today: (1) Songs: folk songs people sang while working, traveling, or remembering home. (2) Legends: old stories told and retold; a tall tale like Paul Bunyan is about a made-up hero. (3) Poems: short writing that captures how a place feels. (4) News: true reports about what happened in a place. (5) Stories that belong to a people, like Hopi stories, told in families for generations. Model an example: "The folk song 'Home on the Range' talks about wide open grassland, buffalo, and a big sky. Just from that song, you can feel the plains of Kansas, in the Midwest." Remind students about courtesy. 3 to 5 minutes.

Five Stories from the Five Regions

The Listening Station holds five stories today: a song, a legend, a poem, a news story, and stories from the Hopi people. Each one comes from a different region. As you read each card, ask yourself: What does this story tell me about the place it comes from?

🎵 STORY 1 · A FOLK SONG · MIDWEST
A famous old American folk song called "Home on the Range" describes wide, open grassland where buffalo roam, deer and antelope play, and the sky is big and clear. A doctor named Brewster Higley wrote the words in Kansas around 1873. Cowboys sang it on long cattle drives. It became the state song of Kansas in 1947.

What's this song about? Wide open land, animals, big skies, and ranching life on the plains.

🪵 STORY 2 · A TALL TALE LEGEND · WEST
The legend of Paul Bunyan tells of a made-up giant logger and his big blue ox, Babe. Loggers in the Pacific Northwest told tall tales about Paul in their camps, among some of the tallest trees on Earth. One tale says Paul dug out Puget Sound in Washington.

What's this legend about? Giant forests, hard work, and the big imagination of logging camps.

🪵 How Paul Bunyan Dug Puget Sound

Long ago, say the loggers, Paul Bunyan came west to the giant forests of Washington. He was so tall that clouds tangled in his beard, and his ox, Babe, was so big and so blue that her footprints filled up with rain and became lakes.

One day Paul decided the logs needed a water road to the sea. So he hitched Babe to a giant scoop and dug, and dug, and dug. Seawater rushed in and filled the long, winding hole. That, the loggers say, is how Puget Sound was made. And all the dirt Paul tossed over his shoulder? It piled up so high it became Mount Rainier!

Traditional tall tale, retold for the Listening Station. Paul Bunyan was never a real person. Loggers told stories like this in their camps in the Pacific Northwest and the north woods, to make long, hard workdays feel lighter.

📜 STORY 3 · A POEM · NORTHEAST
A poet named Emma Lazarus wrote a poem in New York City in 1883 called "The New Colossus." In the poem, the Statue of Liberty welcomes people from across the ocean who are looking for a new home. Today the poem hangs on a plaque inside the statue.

What's this poem about? New York Harbor, the Statue of Liberty, and how it felt to arrive in America.

📜 What the poem says

In "The New Colossus," the Statue of Liberty speaks to the whole world. She is not a warrior statue. She is a "Mother of Exiles," a mother to people who had to leave their homes.

She calls across the ocean: send me people who are tired and poor and want to be free. I will lift my lamp beside the "golden door," the way into America.

Emma Lazarus wrote the poem in New York City in 1883 (public domain). In 1903, it was put on a plaque inside the statue's base, near Ellis Island.

📰 STORY 4 · A NEWS STORY · SOUTHEAST
A 1965 newspaper headline read: "Hurricane Betsy Pounds Louisiana Coast." The story told how floodwater filled neighborhoods in New Orleans, and how neighbors with boats and Coast Guard helicopters rescued people from rooftops.

What's this news about? Hurricane country, and how Gulf Coast neighbors help each other when big storms hit.

The Gulf Coast Times
★ Serving Louisiana and the Coast ★
Vol. 86 New Orleans, La. · Saturday, September 11, 1965 Price: 10¢
HURRICANE BETSY POUNDS LOUISIANA COAST
Floodwaters Rise in New Orleans · Neighbors Help Neighbors

Hurricane Betsy roared ashore late Thursday night, September 9, with winds of about 130 miles per hour. Floodwater covered large parts of southeast Louisiana, including the Lower Ninth Ward of New Orleans. Families who had been told to leave took shelter in schools and churches.

By Friday morning, fishermen along the bayou had launched their boats to rescue neighbors stranded on rooftops. Coast Guard helicopters flew low over the water and lifted people to safety.

Thousands of homes had no electricity. Gifts of canned food, dry clothes, and clean water poured in from across the South. Schools stayed closed through the weekend.

Note: This is a pretend newspaper page, written for the Listening Station. Hurricane Betsy was a real storm. It hit Louisiana on September 9, 1965, and neighbors, fishermen, and the Coast Guard really did rescue people.

🌵 STORY 5 · A STORY FROM THE HOPI PEOPLE · SOUTHWEST
The Hopi people have lived in villages on the mesas of northern Arizona for about 1,000 years. Hopi families tell stories about Spider Grandmother, a wise helper, and pass them down from grandparents to children.

Why listen with care? These stories belong to the Hopi people. We listen, we learn, and we do not change them.

The Big Picture
🏷️ Exhibit Label

Places are understood through more than maps. Songs, legends, poems, and news show us how people feel about where they live: what they love, what they fear, what they remember, and what they celebrate. Together, maps and stories make a region come alive in the visitor's mind.

Quick Self-Check
🧠

Not graded. Just for you! Answer these three questions before you write the Listening Station sign.

1. What can a song or a poem tell us about a place that a map cannot?
AThe exact distance between two cities
BHow people feel about the place: what they love, miss, or remember
CThe names of every state
Right! Maps show where. Stories show how people feel about a place.
Distances and state names are what maps are good at. Think about what makes a song or poem powerful: it's about feelings.
2. The Paul Bunyan tall tale about a giant logger most likely comes from which kind of region?
AA region with huge forests and logging camps
BA hot desert region
CA region near the ocean only
Yes! Paul Bunyan tales grew up in logging camps in giant forests, like the Pacific Northwest. Paul was made up, but the forests were real.
A giant lumberjack story belongs in a place with lots of big trees. Think about which kind of region has those forests.
3. Why is courtesy important when we listen to stories from a region?
ABecause we should pick favorites and ignore the rest
BBecause old stories are not true
CBecause the stories belong to real people who created and shared them
Exactly! Every story comes from real people. Courtesy means we listen first, treat the stories with care, and never say one region's stories are "better" than another's.
A good curator never ignores stories or says one region is better. Think about whose stories these are.

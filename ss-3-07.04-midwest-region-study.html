🏛️ Virtue Spotlight: Courtesy
Some people call the Midwest “flyover country,” as if there is nothing to see. That is not very kind, and it is not true. Courtesy means we look closer and study every region the same way: with real curiosity. The Midwest grows much of the food Americans eat every day. That’s worth understanding carefully.

Where Is the Midwest?
📖
Land, Water, and Climate of the Midwest
A walk through the Heartland before you map it

The Midwest sits in the middle of the country. It includes 12 states: Ohio, Michigan, Indiana, Illinois, Wisconsin, Minnesota, Iowa, Missouri, North Dakota, South Dakota, Nebraska, and Kansas.

🌾 The land. The Midwest is famous for its flat, wide Interior Plains. The western part is called the Great Plains. Long ago, most of this land was prairie: a sea of tall grass with deep roots that built rich, dark soil. This flatness is what makes the region perfect for farming. The land rolls gently in some places (Ohio, southern Illinois) and is almost entirely flat in others (Kansas, North Dakota).

🌊 The water. The Great Lakes (Superior, Michigan, Huron, Erie, and Ontario) sit on the northern edge of the Midwest. They are not salty like the ocean. These five enormous lakes hold about one fifth of all the fresh water on top of the Earth, and millions of people drink their water. Big ships carry iron, coal, and grain across them. The Mississippi River flows through the middle of the region from Minnesota south toward the Gulf. The Missouri River crosses the western plains and joins the Mississippi near St. Louis, Missouri, home of the giant Gateway Arch.

❄️ The climate. The Midwest has dramatic seasons. Winters are harsh: very cold and snowy in the northern states. Summers are hot and humid, perfect for growing corn and soybeans. Tornadoes are common across the central plains in spring.

🌽 The resources. The Midwest is called the "Breadbasket of America" because it grows enormous amounts of corn, soybeans, wheat, and other grains. Iowa and Illinois alone grow more corn than most countries in the world. Most of it is field corn, used for animal feed, foods, and fuel, not the sweet corn people eat on the cob. Wisconsin is famous for milk and cheese. The Midwest is not only farms: it builds things too, like cars in Michigan and steel in Ohio and Indiana, and ships them on rivers, lakes, and railroads. Chicago (Illinois) is the largest city in the Midwest and a major transportation hub. Detroit (Michigan) is known for the automobile industry.

🦸

Midwest Hero: Jesse Owens grew up in Cleveland, Ohio. At the 1936 Olympics in Germany, he won four gold medals for the United States. He showed the whole world what an American athlete could do.

⏳

Then & Now: Long ago, farmers crossed the Midwest on the Oregon Trail, crossing wide, flat grasslands on their way west. Today the Midwest’s farms use GPS-guided tractors, drones, and satellite maps to plant and harvest millions of acres of corn and soybeans every year. What stayed the same about why the Midwest’s flat land and rich soil matter?

🔗

Cause → Choice → Consequence:

  • Cause: The Midwest’s flat land has rich, dark soil, built over thousands of years as prairie grass grew, died, and rotted into the ground.
  • Choice: Settlers plowed the prairie and planted crops on an enormous scale.
  • Consequence: The Midwest became the main food-growing region of the country, earning the name “Breadbasket of America.”

The Big Picture
🏷️ Exhibit Label

The Midwest is the wide, flat heartland of the United States. Its rich soil, the Great Lakes, and the Mississippi River make it one of the greatest farming regions in the world. From the cornfields of Iowa to the auto factories of Michigan, the Midwest shapes how Americans eat, travel, and work.

🦉
🧭 Opti says…
Here’s something wild: Illinois and Iowa grow enormous amounts of corn, but most of it is NOT sweet corn you’d eat on the cob. Most Midwest corn is “field corn,” used for animal feed, ethanol fuel, and making corn syrup. Your bag of chips, your soda, and the gas in a car might all have Midwest corn in them!

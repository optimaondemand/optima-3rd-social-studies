This Unit at a Glance

Last unit looked at all of North America. This unit zooms in on one country. Geographers group the 50 states into five regions: the Northeast, the Southeast, the Midwest, the Southwest, and the West. States in a region are alike in their land, their weather, their resources, and the ways people live. Your student opens one region at a time, then adds two last rooms: songs, legends, poems, and news from each region, and the people from many regions and backgrounds who helped shape the country.

Geography skill
Find the five regions on a U.S. map. Northeast: upper right. Southeast: lower right. Midwest: the middle. Southwest: lower middle, next to Mexico. West: the left side along the Pacific Ocean, plus Alaska and Hawaii.
Virtue lens
Courtesy in every lesson: these are real places where real people live. Your student describes each region with kind, careful words, never “better” or “worse,” and treats every story and every person’s work with respect.
Big idea
Land, stories, and people. Each region is distinct, and every region adds something to the whole country. The last sign in the gallery says it: the United States is shaped by its land, its stories, and its people.

“The United States is shaped by its land, its stories, and its people.”The big idea on the last sign in the gallery, Lesson 3.07.08

Good to know: The lessons correct a few common mix-ups. Alaska and Hawaii sit in small boxes on most U.S. maps, but they are really far away: Alaska is past Canada and is more than twice the size of Texas, and Hawaii is far out in the Pacific Ocean. A map can’t show everything about a place; stories and songs show what people love and remember. Paul Bunyan was never a real person: he comes from a tall tale. People don’t always stay in one region: Sequoyah was born in Tennessee and later lived in Oklahoma. And contributors are not only famous people: in the last lesson your student writes about someone from their own family or community.

In the Museum

The 3rd grade course is set in a museum. In this unit your student is a United States Region Curator, a museum worker who takes care of the regions of the United States and tells visitors about them. Opti the owl is the museum guide.

A new room opens across the hall: the United States Regions Gallery, with five empty alcoves (small rooms along one wall). In Week 19, your student finds their own state on a map, lights up all five regions, and opens the Northeast alcove. In Week 20, the Southeast and the Midwest alcoves open, and in Week 21 the Southwest and the West. When the West sign goes up, all five alcoves glow and your student earns the Region Guide badge. In Week 22, a Listening Station with an old radio and a tape player joins the gallery, and then the Contributions Wall. When your student cuts the ribbon on the wall, the whole gallery lights up, room by room, and they earn the Gallery Founder badge.

Lesson by Lesson

LessonWhat your student learnsWhat they write
3.07.01Week 19 The Five Regions of the United States
Many maps divide the United States into five regions. Geographers put states together when they share the same kind of land, weather, resources, and ways of life. LobbyFind your own state on a map of all 50 states and watch its region light up. GalleryLay four see-through sheets on a light table, one at a time (land, weather, resources, and where people live), then watch the five region lines draw themselves. Study a NASA photo of the United States at night. Hang ten real photos of landmarks, two for each region, in the right alcove. Source: the United States at night (NASA)Lens: Courtesy		 Label the five regions on a U.S. map in the notebook, 1 evidence question, and the Gallery Directory sign: “A region is a group of ___ that share the same ___,” plus one sentence of their own about one region.
3.07.02Week 19 The Northeast
The Northeast sits in the upper right, along the Atlantic Ocean. It has the old, rounded Appalachian Mountains, harbors and lighthouses, four clear seasons, maple syrup, and coal. New York Harbor and Ellis Island welcomed millions of new arrivals. LobbyUnpack the Atlantic Coast crate (a lobster trap, a maple leaf, coal, and a lighthouse) and pin each one on its state. GalleryTurn a season wheel on a Vermont hillside and see when the maple sap runs. Compare two photos of New York Harbor, about 1912 and 2017. Run time forward to see why the Appalachians are rounder than the Rockies. Source: New York Harbor, about 1912 and 2017Lens: Courtesy		 Label the Northeast map, 1 evidence question, and the Northeast alcove label: one sentence of their own about what makes the Northeast distinct.
3.07.03Week 20 The Southeast
The Southeast sits in the lower right, between the Atlantic Ocean and the Gulf of Mexico. Winters are mild and summers are hot and humid. Hurricanes form over warm ocean water, mostly from June to November. Florida’s Everglades is a huge wetland. LobbyWeather twins: the same January day in Boston and in Miami. Then open the Gulf & Atlantic crate (oranges, peanuts, the Florida coast, and Everglades water). GalleryWarm the ocean month by month and track a hurricane as it grows over warm water and weakens over land. Study a photo from an Everglades boardwalk. Make it rain on a paved city and on a wetland, and see which one floods. Source: the Everglades boardwalk (National Park Service)Lens: Courtesy		 1 evidence question and Twin Labels: two sentences, one way the Northeast and the Southeast are alike and one way they are different.
3.07.04Week 20 The Midwest
The Midwest is the middle of the country, called the Heartland and the Breadbasket. Its flat land and rich soil grow huge amounts of corn, soybeans, and wheat. The five Great Lakes hold fresh water that millions of people drink. LobbyScan four foods and things from a store shelf, like cereal and cheese, and see which Midwest state each one came from. GalleryLook out a plane window at three heights over the farms. Study a photo of Chicago on Lake Michigan. Fill the five Great Lakes in order, with a memory trick for their names: HOMES (Huron, Ontario, Michigan, Erie, Superior). Source: Chicago from Lake MichiganLens: Courtesy		 Label the Midwest map, 1 evidence question, and the Heartland Label: one sentence of their own about why the Midwest is called the Heartland.
3.07.05Week 21 The Southwest
The Southwest is in the lower middle, next to Mexico. Much of it is dry desert with mesas and canyons. The Colorado River carved the Grand Canyon over millions of years. Pueblo, Navajo, and Hopi nations have lived here for a very long time, along with Mexican-American families. LobbyWatch three jars fill with one year of rain and pick the one from Phoenix, Arizona (only about 7 inches). Then open the Desert & Canyon crate. GalleryRun time forward and watch a river carve a canyon through rock layers. Study a photo of Acoma Pueblo, a village on a mesa where people still live. Test a thin-walled house and a thick adobe house at noon and at midnight. Source: Acoma Pueblo, New MexicoLens: Courtesy		 The Southwest Alcove Sign: 3 sentences about where the Southwest is, what the land looks like, and one thing about the people who live there.
3.07.06Week 21 The West
The West runs along the Pacific Ocean and through the Rocky Mountains, and it includes Alaska and Hawaii. Alaska is the biggest state. Hawaii is made of volcanic islands. Mountains can make one side wet and the other side dry (a rain shadow). LobbyDrag Alaska and Hawaii out of their little map boxes to where they really are. GalleryBlow wind over flat land, then raise the mountains and see the rain shadow. Study a photo of lava from Kīlauea pouring into the sea. Slide the ocean floor over a hot spot and watch the Hawaiian islands grow. Source: Kīlauea, Hawaii (U.S. Geological Survey)Lens: Courtesy		 The West Alcove Sign (3 sentences, naming Alaska and Hawaii) and a Gallery Plaque: one pattern all five regions share, one big difference, and an example. This earns the Region Guide badge.
3.07.07Week 22 Songs, Legends, Poems, and News
Stories tell what a map can’t: what people love, miss, and remember about a place. The lesson has a song (“Home on the Range,” Midwest), a tall tale (Paul Bunyan, West), a poem (“The New Colossus” at the Statue of Liberty, Northeast), the news (Hurricane Betsy, 1965, Southeast), and a story from the Hopi people (Southwest). LobbyTune an old radio across the country and guess each song’s region from real old recordings. GalleryPlay five story tapes and match each to its region. Study a photo of a rescue after Hurricane Betsy, then unfold a newspaper and find what, when, where, and who. Source: a 1965 Coast Guard rescue photoLens: Courtesy		 The Listening Station Sign: 3 sentences that name a kind of story, one story they remember, and one story from their own region.
3.07.08Week 22 Contributions from Diverse Groups
A contributor is a person whose work helps others and shapes a place. The lesson meets six: Sequoyah (a writing system for the Cherokee language), Frederick Douglass (spoke and wrote against slavery), Maya Lin (designed the Vietnam Veterans Memorial), Sandra Day O’Connor (first woman on the Supreme Court), Cesar Chavez (brought farm workers together to ask for fair pay), and Queen Liliʻuokalani (the last queen of Hawaii). LobbyLift the cloth on six objects and guess who made each one. GalleryHang the Contributions Wall and watch gold threads show where each person lived and moved. Study the first Cherokee Phoenix newspaper (1828), then tap Sequoyah’s symbols to write “Cherokee” and “hello.” Source: the Cherokee Phoenix, 1828Lens: Courtesy		 The Contributions Wall Closing Sign (naming at least one contributor) and a small plaque for one person from their own region, famous or from their family or community. Cutting the ribbon earns the Gallery Founder badge.

Words to Know

Use them at home!
regiona group of states that are alike in many ways, like their land, weather, resources, or ways of life
geographera person who studies the earth and its places
climatethe usual weather of a place over many years
distinctdifferent in its own way
hurricanea huge storm with very strong winds and heavy rain that forms over warm ocean water
wetlandland covered by shallow water and plants for much of the year, like the Everglades
Great Lakesfive huge freshwater lakes on the northern edge of the Midwest: Superior, Michigan, Huron, Erie, and Ontario
prairiewide, flat land covered with tall grass
desertland that gets very little rain. Many deserts are rocky, not sandy.
canyon / mesaa deep valley with steep sides / a tall hill with a flat top and steep sides
adobea brick made of mud and straw, dried in the sun
volcanoan opening in the Earth where lava, ash, and gas come out
rain shadowthe dry side of a mountain range, where little rain falls
legend / tall talean old story people tell again and again / a funny legend that stretches the truth
contributora person whose work helps others and shapes a place
syllabarya writing system where each symbol stands for a whole sound, like Sequoyah’s for the Cherokee language

Ask at Home

Better than “How was school?”
  1. Which region do we live in? How do you know?Listen for: the region’s name, and something about its land, weather, or resources that fits.
  2. Can you name all five regions?Listen for: the Northeast, Southeast, Midwest, Southwest, and West.
  3. Where does a hurricane get its strength?Listen for: warm ocean water. Over land it gets weaker.
  4. Why is the Midwest called the Breadbasket?Listen for: its flat land and rich soil grow huge amounts of corn, wheat, and other grains.
  5. How did the Grand Canyon get so deep?Listen for: the Colorado River carved it a little at a time, over millions of years.
  6. Where are Alaska and Hawaii really?Listen for: Alaska is far north, past Canada, and Hawaii is far out in the Pacific Ocean. They only look close on maps with little boxes.
  7. Was Paul Bunyan a real person?Listen for: no. He is from a tall tale, a story that stretches the truth.
  8. Who is someone from our region whose work helped other people?Listen for: a name and what they did. It can be a famous person or someone your student knows.

Try at Home

5 minutes
  • Our region. On a U.S. map, find your state, then its region. Name one neighbor state in the same region.
  • Where did breakfast come from? Pick one food in your kitchen and guess which region grew it.
  • A song about a place. Sing or listen to a song about your state or town. What does it say people love about it?
  • A family contributor. Tell your student about someone in your family or community whose work helped others.
